    03Case studies

    Three problems, start to finish.

    Case study 01 · Product analytics · Darwinbox

    Low adoption that pointed to a findability problem

    The problem
    Core had no behavioural analytics. Pendo had been bought, but two or three people used it only to send surveys, so after a release nobody could say whether a feature was being used. Roadmap calls ran on escalations and memory.
    What I did
    I took Pendo on, defined a tagging taxonomy and tagged 700+ features, then built funnel and path views over the key Core flows: imports, lifecycle changes and settings.
    The call
    Several features had poor adoption, and the instinctive read was that clients did not want them, so build something new. The path data pointed to a different explanation: people entered nearby flows but never reached those features. I treated that as a findability problem and prioritised plain-language setting names, help text, and settings moved to where admins actually look.
    What happened
    We shipped clearer names, help text and a reorganisation of existing settings instead of a replacement feature build, and Pendo showed usage of those settings climbing once admins could find and understand them. The cost was a UI reorganisation rather than a redundant build cycle. The same approach informed the broader settings audit described in Work.

    Case study 02 · Localisation · Darwinbox

    Translations that were correct and still wrong

    The problem
    Localisation looked solved and kept breaking. Strings were translated one at a time and hard-coded, and native-speaking clients kept flagging translations that were literally correct but made no sense in context.
    The first fix
    Leadership brought in an architect and centralised translations on an internal page, and considered the work complete. For client-facing teams it got harder: each client's page sat behind a hidden link nobody owned, strings were scattered across PHP and Angular files, and routine updates still needed an engineer.
    The call
    The alternative was to keep patching strings one by one as clients raised them. Because I knew how the settings and their dependencies connected, I could see the real problem was the workflow and its ownership gaps, not individual bad translations. I mapped every issue, raised it, and when it became a roadmap item I owned the PRD end to end. I did not design the page from scratch; I took a workflow that did not work and made it work.
    What happened
    Translation updates became a product-team operation with no engineering involvement per cycle, and turnaround went from waiting for a sprint to the same release cycle, across 10+ languages.

    Case study 03 · Strategy · XLRI Jamshedpur

    An IPO-readiness deck where every number had to survive a question

    The problem
    Our team assessed for a panel whether Aster DM Healthcare's Gulf business was organisationally ready for an IPO. The argument rested on listed-peer comparisons, regulation and penalty figures, and much of that first turned up in secondary sources.
    What I did
    Before we presented, I traced every figure in our deck to a primary or named source, with one rule: anything that could not be traced was removed, not softened.
    The call
    The hardest catch was two real, sourced numbers sitting side by side: one listed peer's share-price decline since its IPO next to another's twelve-month decline. Both were true and the comparison was false, so I found the second peer's offer price and restated both on the same basis. The same pass replaced a penalty figure taken from the wrong company-size band and cut a legal claim that went further than the law does.
    What happened
    Eight errors fixed before the panel, and a top 3 finish out of 20 teams. Where no defensible number existed, such as what a readiness programme would cost, the deck named who should own it instead of inventing a figure.

    06Ask me

    Four questions I get asked.

    CareerWhy move from civil engineering to software?

    Civil engineering is still close to me. Most of my family works in it, and I still enjoy seeing how things get built. At placements Darwinbox came to campus early, and I applied out of curiosity. I cleared the interview without knowing how software was made, and that told me the part of civil engineering I was best at, planning a build in detail before it starts, would carry over. A PRD does for a software team what a good drawing does for a site. I moved to a field where I could use that skill on something new.

    CareerWhy an MBA after 45 months at Darwinbox?

    I had stopped growing in the role. When I watched my manager and director decide something, they were weighing clients, teams and the business at once. I could do that inside a PRD, not beyond it. I could wait years to pick it up, or go and learn it. Both of them studied at XLRI, and they helped me with the application.

    Working styleHow do you keep engineers unblocked?

    I wrote each PRD to answer its own questions: edge cases, what to test and what it might affect. I tracked which developer would pick up which PRD in the coming sprint and scheduled the knowledge transfer before anyone got stuck. Most questions were answered in the document or the handover, so only the genuinely hard calls reached me, and the work closed within office hours.

    JudgementWhat do you do when your own feature does not land?

    Features I owned landed anywhere from 5% to 60% account-level adoption, and one I was proud of sat at the low end. I had half-convinced myself it was a rollout problem. Instead I pulled my own drop-off data and treated the design as the suspect, and the data showed the feature, as designed, was not earning its place. I would rather break my own assumption in a planning room than have clients break it later at a higher cost.
