    03Case studies

    Three problems, start to finish.

    Case study 01 · Product analytics · Darwinbox

    Low adoption that pointed to a findability problem

    The problem
    Pendo was available, but used mainly for surveys. Without consistent feature-level tracking, it was difficult to assess adoption after a release. Roadmap discussions relied heavily on client feedback and escalations.
    What I did
    I took Pendo on, defined a tagging taxonomy and tagged 700+ features, then built funnel and path views over the key Core flows: imports, lifecycle changes and settings.
    The call
    Low adoption could reflect limited demand or difficulty finding a feature. The path data pointed to the latter: people entered nearby flows but never reached those features. I prioritised plain-language setting names, help text and clearer placement before recommending replacement builds.
    What happened
    We shipped clearer names, help text and a reorganisation of existing settings to improve findability and comprehension. The same approach informed the broader settings audit described in Work.

    Case study 02 · Localisation · Darwinbox

    Making localisation work in context

    The problem
    Strings were translated individually and hard-coded. Feedback from native-speaking clients showed that some literal translations did not fit the context in which they appeared.
    The foundation
    An earlier centralisation effort brought translations into an internal page and established a shared foundation. Building on that work, I focused on a workflow for client-facing teams, with clearer navigation, defined access and ownership, and a consistent way to update strings across PHP and Angular.
    The call
    I mapped the recurring issues across settings and their dependencies and raised the workflow improvements for the roadmap. When the work was prioritised, I owned the PRD end to end. I built on the existing approach, focusing on clear access, ownership and update steps so the product team could manage routine translations independently.
    What happened
    Product teams could manage routine translation updates without engineering support each cycle. Turnaround improved from waiting for a sprint to the same release cycle, across 10+ languages.

    Case study 03 · Strategy · XLRI Jamshedpur

    An IPO-readiness deck where every number had to survive a question

    The problem
    Our team assessed for a panel whether Aster DM Healthcare's Gulf business was organisationally ready for an IPO. The argument rested on listed-peer comparisons, regulation and penalty figures, and much of that first turned up in secondary sources.
    What I did
    Before we presented, I traced every figure in our deck to a primary or named source, with one rule: anything that could not be traced was removed, not softened.
    The call
    One check involved two sourced figures: one listed peer's share-price decline since its IPO and another's twelve-month decline. The periods differed, so I found the second peer's offer price and restated both on the same basis. The review also aligned a penalty figure with the applicable company-size band and narrowed a legal claim to what the source supported.
    What happened
    Eight corrections completed before the panel, and a top 3 finish out of 20 teams. Where a figure could not be verified, such as the cost of a readiness programme, the deck identified who would need to assess it.

    06Ask me

    Four questions I get asked.

    CareerWhy move from civil engineering to software?

    Civil engineering is still close to me. Most of my family works in it, and I still enjoy seeing how things get built. Darwinbox came to campus early, and I applied out of curiosity. Moving into product work showed me how the habit of planning a build in detail could carry over to software. A PRD does for a software team what a good drawing does for a site. I found a new setting in which to use that skill.

    CareerWhy an MBA after 45 months at Darwinbox?

    I wanted to build on my product experience and develop a broader business perspective. Working with my manager and director helped me understand how decisions bring client needs, team priorities and business goals together. Both are XLRI alumni and encouraged and supported my application.

    Working styleHow do you keep engineers unblocked?

    I documented edge cases, acceptance criteria and dependencies in each PRD. Before the coming sprint, I coordinated with the developers taking up the work and scheduled a knowledge transfer. The document and handover established a shared understanding, while follow-up discussions focused on decisions that needed clarification.

    JudgementWhat do you do when your own feature does not land?

    Features I owned reached anywhere from 5% to 60% account-level adoption, and one I had high hopes for was at the low end. I initially thought rollout might explain it, then reviewed the drop-off data and reconsidered the design. The evidence did not support the value I had expected it to deliver. I would rather test my assumptions during planning than have clients encounter avoidable limitations after release.
